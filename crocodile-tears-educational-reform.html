The Crocodile Tears of Educational
Reform: Trading Academic Futures
for Political Drama

If you want to understand a society's true priorities, ignore what its leaders say and look at what they optimize for.

✍️ Antony Ancil — Kollam, Kerala 📅 August 2026 ⏱️ 4 min read

A personal opinion piece on the pattern of academic disruption in Kerala, not a comment on any single organization or protest.

In Kerala, there's a recurring, highly predictable drama. Political organizations, student unions, and self-proclaimed "well-wishers of education" routinely take to the streets. They declare strikes, mandate hartals, and shut down the state — under the banner of "educational reform" or "student welfare."

But strip away the performative outrage and look at the underlying numbers, and a real tension emerges: the same disruptions claiming to protect students are eating directly into the one resource education can't function without — instructional time.

📊 The Math of Disruption

Education is a highly structured system. It requires a set number of instructional days to properly move a syllabus from paper into a student's head.

WHERE THE NUMBERS ACTUALLY STAND

Kerala's stated target (recent years)~220 working days
Revised mandate, 2025–26 (High Court–driven)198–204 days, by level

Figures per Kerala General Education Department academic calendar announcements and Kerala High Court proceedings, 2024–25.

You can't compress a full syllabus into fewer working days than the calendar was designed for and expect the same outcome. When a day is lost to a strike, teachers rush through complex material, and students absorb the resulting stress along with a thinner version of the subject. The system strains under the compression.

🎭 Performative Politics, or Reform?

This raises a genuinely pragmatic question: are these strikes actually designed to reform education and uplift students?

My honest read: rarely, when the calendar itself is the thing being disrupted. If the underlying goal is genuine educational improvement, the academic calendar is close to the last thing you'd want to touch.

In my view, many of these hartals function less as tools of reform and more as a demonstration of political reach — a way for parties and unions to assert relevance, control a narrative, or apply pressure on an opposing side. Students often end up bearing the disruption without being the actual intended audience of the political message.

"Concern for a student's future rings hollow the moment their classroom becomes the battlefield."

🏗️ The Systemic Cost

A society that treats its academic calendar as a negotiable, expendable resource is making a real trade-off, even if it's rarely named out loud. Just as physical resources get wasted in inefficient systems, instructional time — a genuinely finite, non-renewable resource for any given student's education — gets spent on disruption instead of learning.

A hartal that closes schools is, functionally, a systemic cost paid by people who didn't choose to spend it that way.

🧭 What Real Reform Would Look Like

If the goal is genuine improvement, the more honest starting point is naming the trade-off plainly: disruptions that close schools carry a real cost to the students they're framed as protecting, whatever the stated intent.

Real reform, in my view, doesn't happen by shutting institutions down. It happens by keeping them open, insulating the academic calendar from political conflict as much as realistically possible, and treating instructional time as close to untouchable infrastructure.

Every day lost to a hartal is a day a student doesn't get back.
Whatever the politics, that ledger doesn't balance itself.
