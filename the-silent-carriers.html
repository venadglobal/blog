The Silent Carriers: What Our
Insults Say About Human Ego

We use the names of our two greatest allies as our worst insults. It doesn't expose their flaws. It exposes ours.

✍️ Antony Ancil — Kollam, Kerala 📅 August 2026 ⏱️ 4 min read

When human beings reach the absolute limit of their patience — when they want to deeply insult someone's character, intelligence, or worth — they almost instinctively reach for the names of two specific creatures.

They call each other a dog. Or they call each other a donkey.

It's a global phenomenon. Across languages and cultures, these two words are weaponized to mean someone is dirty, treacherous, stupid, or worthless.

But strip away the human ego and look at the historical and biological facts, and a tragic irony surfaces: we're using the names of our two greatest allies as our worst insults. It exposes something about human behavioral patterns — the psychology of "use and throw" culture.

🏗️ The Architect and the Guardian

Consider what these two animals have actually done for humanity.

The Donkey — Physical Carrier

For thousands of years, the donkey literally carried human civilization on its back — moving the stones that built early cities, carrying the water that sustained our ancestors, working the soil that fed us. Even today, in blistering heat, they silently pull impossible loads in developing nations.

The Dog — Emotional Carrier

If the donkey built our physical world, the dog secured our emotional and tactical one. Dogs guarded our fires in the dark so early humans could finally sleep, evolved to read human facial expressions, and absorbed our grief — giving near-total trust and asking for nothing but our presence.

Yet we use the donkey's name to mean "stupid." The famous "stubbornness" of a donkey isn't stupidity at all — it's a highly developed sense of self-preservation. A horse, bred and evolved for open-plain flight, might panic and bolt off a cliff; a donkey will stop, assess the environment, and refuse to move if it senses real danger. Humans mislabeled that as "stubbornness" simply because the animal refused to be bullied into a dangerous situation.

And across the world, we use "dog" to describe someone disloyal or treacherous — projecting our own capacity for betrayal onto the one creature biologically incapable of it.

♻️ The Psychology of "Use and Throw"

Why do we curse the very creatures that carry our physical and emotional burdens? The answer lies in a flawed human behavioral pattern: a lack of respect for unconditional service.

In human society, value often gets tied to leverage. If someone has power, money, or the ability to walk away, they get respect. But the dog and the donkey don't walk away. They stay. They endure. They give their labor and loyalty unconditionally.

Instead of recognizing that as a rare virtue, the human ego too often treats it as a weakness to exploit. That's the root of the "use and throw" pattern that shows up across modern human dynamics.

The loyal employee who builds a company for 20 years, only to be discarded during downsizing without a second thought.

The friend or partner drained for their emotional support, then quietly dropped once they're no longer "useful."

The political systems that ride the genuine outrage of citizens to gain power, only to forget them the moment the election is won.

Humanity has a habit of extracting maximum value from the silent, the loyal, and the hardworking — and then treating them with contempt precisely because they don't fight back.

"When we use these animals as insults, it doesn't expose their flaws. It exposes ours."

🪞 The Mirror

Silently, they carry on. They continue hauling our loads and guarding our homes, ignoring insults they don't understand, executing their purpose with a quiet dignity most humans never quite reach.

The next time someone reaches for "dog" or "donkey" to insult another person's character, it's worth sitting with the irony for a second.

If a human being actually possessed the unwavering integrity of a dog
and the stoic endurance of a donkey,
it wouldn't be an insult.

It would be a leader.
