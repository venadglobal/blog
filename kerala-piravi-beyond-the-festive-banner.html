Beyond the Festive Banner: The Radical Call for Self-Assessment

As Kerala marks its formation day, it is worth looking beyond maps, speeches and familiar measures of success—and asking how honestly we live with ourselves and with one another.

✍️ Antony Ancil · VGC Reflection📅 November 2026🌐 Kerala, India⏱️ 7 minutes read
VGC REFLECTION NOTE: This article is a personal civic and social reflection. It is not an allegation against every public servant, artist, journalist, voter or institution, nor an endorsement of any political party. Its central appeal is accountability—beginning with ourselves.

Every November 1st, Kerala Piravi invites us to celebrate the formation of our state. We see traditional attire, cultural programmes, public messages and expressions of pride. We speak of literacy, social development and the achievements of Malayalis across the world. These are meaningful reasons to celebrate. But celebration can also give us an opportunity to ask a more demanding question: are we becoming the society we believe ourselves to be?

A map can define a territory, and statistics can describe parts of its progress. Neither can fully measure the honesty, compassion or everyday integrity of the people who live there. A state is shaped not only by its institutions, but also by the choices made in homes, workplaces, public spaces and civic life.

🎭 The Trap of External Metrics: Money, Fame and Status

Modern life often encourages us to measure success through visible markers:

Wealth

How much have we accumulated, and what does that wealth say about the way we earned it?

Visibility

How widely are we noticed, followed or praised—and how much of that attention is genuine?

Status

What title or position do we hold, and do our actions deserve the respect attached to it?

Authenticity

Are we the same person in private that we present to the world in public?

There is nothing inherently wrong with wealth, recognition or professional achievement. The problem begins when the pursuit of these things becomes more important than the principles that should guide us. In everyday life, that can appear as convenient compromises, petty rivalries, backbiting, false appearances or the habit of agreeing publicly while acting differently in private.

Looking successful is not the same as living with integrity. The first can be performed; the second has to be practised.

🏛️ The Civic Question: Corruption, Cynicism and the Ballot Box

Public trust is one of the foundations of a healthy society. Whenever people encounter allegations of corruption, delays, favouritism, opaque decisions or unequal treatment, confidence in institutions can weaken. These concerns deserve scrutiny, evidence and fair investigation—not automatic acceptance, but not casual dismissal either.

There is also a contradiction worth examining. Citizens may openly criticise political systems and express frustration with governance, yet election choices can sometimes reproduce familiar patterns. That does not mean every voter is careless or every repeated choice is irrational. People vote for different reasons: local needs, policy preferences, identity, leadership, past performance, personal experience or the options available to them.

The deeper civic question is whether we examine those reasons honestly. Do we evaluate candidates and policies with care? Do we expect transparency from the people we elect? Do we hold our preferred side to the same standards we apply to its opponents? Accountability loses its meaning when it is demanded only from those we already disagree with.

🎙️ The Responsibility of Institutions, Media and Public Figures

Public institutions, the media, artists and well-known cultural figures have influence beyond their immediate roles. That influence can strengthen public understanding, encourage empathy and bring attention to important issues. It can also be used carelessly—or remain silent when thoughtful engagement would matter.

It would be unfair to label an entire bureaucracy, media community or film industry as corrupt or hypocritical. Many people within these fields work with commitment and integrity, often under difficult conditions. At the same time, no profession should be beyond scrutiny. Public office calls for impartial service; journalism calls for accuracy and independence; cultural influence carries a responsibility to avoid misleading audiences or exploiting public trust.

Silence is not automatically proof of wrongdoing, and public criticism should be based on verifiable conduct rather than assumptions about private motives. But where influence is used to shape public opinion, reasonable questions about transparency, consistency and accountability are entirely legitimate.

⚖️ A fair standard

Criticise documented actions, decisions and conflicts of interest. Avoid treating an entire profession or community as guilty because of the conduct of particular individuals. The demand for integrity must itself be fair and evidence-based.

🪞 The Core Question: Are We Truthful to Ourselves?

Before pointing fingers at leaders, officials, public figures, colleagues or neighbours, we should be willing to turn the mirror towards ourselves. A personal audit is not an excuse for institutions to escape accountability. It is the recognition that public standards are strengthened when individuals practise the values they demand from others.

Are you living in alignment with your values, or performing a character designed mainly to please the crowd?
When nobody is watching, do you still choose what is right—even when doing so is inconvenient?
Do you refuse to participate in gossip, unfairness or favouritism, even when those habits are socially rewarded?
Do you apply the same standards of honesty to the people and political positions you support as you apply to those you oppose?
Are you willing to correct your own mistakes, acknowledge uncomfortable facts and change a harmful habit?

🌱 What Needs to Change?

The question is not simply how Kerala should present itself to the world tomorrow. It is also what each of us can improve today. Personal integrity and institutional reform are not competing ideas. We need both.

At home and at work

Keep promises, treat people fairly, acknowledge mistakes and resist the temptation to gain through another person's disadvantage.

In public life

Check information before sharing it, ask for evidence, use lawful channels to raise concerns and support transparent processes.

As voters and citizens

Evaluate performance, policies and credible information. Question every side consistently and participate beyond election day.

As a community

Make room for disagreement without personal abuse, protect fairness and recognise people who act with integrity.

Real transformation does not begin only in a legislative assembly, a government office, a newsroom or a film set. It also begins in quiet decisions: refusing a bribe, telling the truth, correcting misinformation, treating a worker with dignity, or admitting that our own side was wrong. These actions do not replace systemic reform; they help create the public culture that makes reform possible.

This Kerala Piravi, let us celebrate what our state has achieved—but let us also have the courage to examine what still needs to change. Before asking whether Kerala is honest with us, let each of us ask whether we are honest with Kerala, with one another, and with ourselves.

A better society begins not with a perfect public image, but with the courage to practise the values we claim to believe in.
