The Outrage Economy: Why Legacy
Politics Cannot Fix Modern Crises

Systemic failures require systemic fixes. Time is the one thing modern politics refuses to allow.

✍️ Antony Ancil — Kollam, Kerala 📅 July 2026 ⏱️ 4 min read

A personal opinion piece on Indian political tactics, written against the backdrop of the 2026 NEET-UG controversy — not a comment on the underlying legal proceedings, which remain active.

When a massive, nationwide system falters — such as the 2026 NEET examination controversy — the public reaction is immediate, emotional, and loud. The anxiety of millions of students is entirely justified. A structural breach of this magnitude demands real accountability.

WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED

The NEET-UG exam held on May 3, 2026, for over 2.27 million aspirants, was cancelled on May 12 after investigators found a pre-circulated guess paper overlapped with the actual questions. Several people were arrested, including insiders connected to the National Testing Agency. A CBI investigation and Supreme Court petitions followed. This wasn't a manufactured grievance — the failure was real, and the anger it triggered was earned.

There's a cold, executive truth that gets lost in the noise of the streets, though: systemic failures require systemic fixes, and rebuilding a compromised bureaucratic machine takes time, precision, and space. In the landscape of modern politics, time is the one thing the opposition rarely seems willing to allow.

⚙️ The Weaponization of Chaos

When an administration is working to audit, secure, and fix a compromised system, the role of a responsible opposition is constructive oversight — pressing for accountability while the fix is underway. What often shows up instead, in my view, is something closer to an "Outrage Economy" in full effect.

Political factions, in my assessment, are frequently more interested in the next election cycle than in the meticulous, unglamorous work of fixing examination protocols. Hijacking a real, justified grievance for political advantage isn't leadership. It's opportunism riding on the back of genuine student pain.

📱 The Algorithmic Fuel

This dynamic is being accelerated by a newer catalyst: social media algorithms. In today's digital ecosystem, algorithms don't reward nuance or patience — they reward rage. Ground-level frustration, however justified at its root, tends to get amplified fastest by platforms built to push short, high-emotion clips over slower, more complete context.

The real failure (the leak, the arrests, the cancelled exam) is what started the anger. But once it's in the algorithmic bloodstream, the loudest framing usually wins out over the most accurate one — and that makes it harder for anyone, government or opposition, to have a calm, fact-based conversation about the actual fix.

🏛️ The Expiration Date of Dynastic Politics

This pattern points, in my view, to a deeper flaw in legacy political systems generally. For decades, a common political formula relied on a historical family name, control over the flow of information, and the entitlement of the past.

But information asymmetry is largely gone. Rewriting the narrative is much harder when the electorate carries a search engine in their pocket. A political entity that leans mainly on legacy and dynasty rather than present-day execution is, in my assessment, increasingly out of step with how people actually process information now.

"Performance is the only currency that still holds its value. Everything else is just noise."

⚖️ The Verdict

True leadership isn't built on historical entitlement, or on who can shout loudest during a crisis. It's built on the quieter, more methodical work of actually fixing broken systems — while still being honest that the anger driving the crisis was earned in the first place.

The system failed the students first.
The least any side owes them now is a real fix — not just a louder microphone.
